      How it works

      Levels

      Everything starts from C. First the Cs: C5–C7, then C3–C5, then C7–C9, then all seven, from C3 (131 Hz) to C9 (8,372 Hz). Then the same four ranges with F♯ added halfway between the Cs, then E and G♯ (thirds of an octave), then D♯, F♯ and A (quarters), then all twelve notes on a keyboard.

      Every set is learned by note name first, then by number with the note, and each pass goes name, then find, with typing the number in between on the number pass. You name a tone on a full piano keyboard or with buttons; choose in Settings. After a miss, or after any typed or tuned answer, your tone and the right one take turns every two and a half seconds until you go on. Notes are rounded to whole hertz. Last comes any frequency. Every answer, typed or tuned, passes within a quarter tone (about ±2.9%): close enough to say which note a tone is nearest.

      A perfect 10 of 10 moves you up a step; 6 or fewer moves you back one.

      Interference

      Before every question: fast bursts of notes on your instrument, close to what you just heard, alternating with white noise, four times over. It erases the last tone so you can't answer by comparison. Either part can be switched off in Settings.

      Find the tone

      Spin the record until the tone sounds like the number. On an instrument the note sounds every four and a half seconds; turning the record silences it, and letting go plays the new pitch, so you never hear it slide. A pure sine is one continuous tone that follows the record. within a quarter tone (about ±2.9%) passes. The record spans a random two to four octaves with the target anywhere in it, so its ends say nothing about where the target is. The record moves in even steps of 5 cents: the single arrows move one step, the double arrows a half step, the triple arrows an octave, and after each turn the change shows under the target: in cents when the target is a note, in percent when it is a frequency. Where it starts is random, so how far you've turned says nothing about where the target is. Past either end the tone fades out and comes back at the other end. The first Find steps use one tone at a time: C6, then C5, then C7, then all three. Those one-tone rounds start by playing the target once, so you know what you're aiming for; tap Start when you're ready, and the interference before each question clears it again.

      Instruments

      Every tone in a round plays on the instrument in your profile, or as a pure sine wave. Recordings are retuned to the exact number; between notes, the two nearest recordings are retuned and blended, on a grid shifted at random every question, so the sound never marks where the notes are. If your instrument can’t play every tone a round might use, that round is on piano.

      Echo

      A minigame: a tone plays once, then you tune the record back to it. Turn on interference to put it in between, which is much harder. It tests how well you hold a pitch for a few seconds, which is not perfect pitch, so it stays out of your training stats. Each of the five tones scores up to 10 points, one less per 10 cents off. The daily five are the same for everyone; share your first try as a row of squares.

      Memory

      Pull is how far your answers lean toward the tone before them. Cold is the first answer of a round after 10 quiet minutes.

      Long-term memory training

      You just found C6 ten times in a row. But a pitch you haven’t heard for a while fades fast, often within minutes.

      Each time you find it again just before it fades, it comes back stronger and stays longer. That’s spaced repetition, the trick behind flashcard apps like Anki and Duolingo.

      So we’ll check on C6 at the right moments: in a few minutes, then a little longer after every hit, from hours to days to weeks. Miss one, and the next check comes sooner.

      The first week or two are busy: lots of quick checks, under half a minute each. It gets much quieter as C6 sticks.
